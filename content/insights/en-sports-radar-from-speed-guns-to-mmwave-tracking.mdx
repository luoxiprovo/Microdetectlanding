# Why Sports Radar Is Moving from Speed Guns to Millimeter-Wave Imaging and Tracking

A speed reading answers one useful question: how fast was that pitch, serve, or shot? A coach usually has a second question: what changed between this attempt and the last one? Answering it may require the ball’s direction, the part of its flight actually observed, and a repeatable record of the session.

That is the practical reason to move beyond a single speed number. Millimeter-wave radar can contribute to richer tracking systems, but the useful advance comes from the complete measurement chain: antennas, waveform, signal processing, tracking software, installation geometry, and validation. A higher operating frequency alone does not deliver a complete performance analysis.

This first article in our sports-radar series explains that transition and offers a way to evaluate it without confusing a convincing visualization with a verified measurement.

## What a speed gun measures—and what it leaves open

Doppler radar measures motion toward or away from the sensor. This line-of-sight component is called radial velocity. As TI explains in its [range, velocity, and angle primer](https://www.ti.com/lit/ta/sszt906/sszt906.pdf), velocity is one observable alongside distance and direction.

Consider a simplified straight-line shot traveling at 100 km/h. If its direction is 20 degrees away from the radar’s viewing axis, the radial component is about 94 km/h: speed multiplied by the cosine of the angle. This is an illustrative geometry calculation, not a product test. A system may compensate using independently estimated direction, but a basic radial reading cannot supply that missing information by itself.

For a coach comparing sessions, placement therefore matters. Moving the instrument between repetitions can create an apparent performance change. Recording the mounting position, aim, and measurement point makes the results more interpretable.

A simple speed gun can still be the right tool when the goal is a repeatable speed check in a controlled lane. More elaborate tracking is useful when the question requires more evidence.

## What FMCW and multiple antennas add

TI’s [mmWave fundamentals white paper](https://www.ti.com/lit/wp/spyy005a/spyy005a.pdf) describes frequency-modulated continuous-wave radar, or FMCW. It transmits frequency sweeps called chirps. Processing the returns estimates distance; changes across chirps provide velocity information; differences between receiving antennas support angle estimation.

With suitable hardware and processing, these observations can become detections with a position, radial velocity, timestamp, and signal-quality information. A tracker then associates detections over time to estimate a trajectory. Association is an additional inference: the system must decide which returns belong to the same ball rather than to a club, player, net, or another ball.

Short wavelengths allow compact antenna arrangements, but small packaging is not a guarantee of fine angular separation. Bandwidth, antenna aperture, signal strength, configuration, and processing all matter. Nor is the ability to separate nearby targets the same specification as the accuracy of a reported position.

A practical distinction for buyers follows: ask separately about detection, continuous tracking, and the final metric. A device may detect a fast-moving object without retaining a reliable track through the entire action.

## “Imaging” means a spatial representation, not necessarily a picture

In a sports-radar proposal, imaging might mean a spatial map of reflected energy or a collection of detected points. It does not necessarily mean a camera-like image, detailed body pose, or identification of every object in the scene. Ask the supplier to show exactly what the sensor produces before the application draws a polished animation.

Imagine a golf bay that displays a complete flight ending at a predicted landing point. The first section may be observed while the remainder is generated by a flight model. That can be useful for training, provided the interface identifies the observed segment and the modeled segment. Otherwise, users can mistake a calculated outcome for a directly tracked one.

The same discipline applies to spin and launch metrics. Establish which quantities are directly measured, which use sensor fusion, and which are inferred. “Radar-equipped” does not establish that every displayed parameter is measured by radar.

## Commercial sports systems already combine sensing methods

Trackman’s [Trackman 4 product page](https://www.trackman.com/golf/launch-monitors/trackman-4) describes a system combining two radars and a camera. This is a concrete example of sports measurement extending beyond a standalone speed display. It is the manufacturer’s description, not independent verification of its accuracy, and the cited page does not establish a millimeter-wave operating band.

The broader engineering lesson is to choose sensors for the evidence needed. A camera may supply visual context while radar contributes motion measurements. Combining them also introduces synchronization, calibration, and association work. A fused output should be evaluated as a complete system.

There is no universal migration in which every sports radar must become a mmWave imaging device. Legacy frequency bands and simpler sensors remain relevant. The opportunity is to solve a specific training problem with a measurement system that can demonstrate its performance.

## Start with one decision the coach needs to make

For an initial pilot, choose a narrow question: can the system compare ball speed across repetitions in one training lane, or track the early trajectory of a shot in a defined volume? Avoid making a full-venue tracking demonstration the first acceptance target.

Agree on the following before collecting data:

- **Measurement definition:** release speed, peak speed, speed at a specified point, or another explicitly defined quantity.
- **Operating envelope:** ball type, distance, speed range, launch directions, and number of simultaneous targets.
- **Reference method:** an appropriate calibrated comparator with synchronized timing and a matching measurement definition.
- **Success criteria:** acceptable error, valid-track rate, false detections, latency, and repeatability after reinstalling the sensor.
- **Failure behavior:** what appears when the ball is lost, another target is selected, or confidence falls below the agreed threshold.

Test both ordinary repetitions and deliberately difficult cases. Include a player crossing the sensing area, off-axis shots, nearby moving equipment, and multiple balls if those conditions belong to the intended use. Retain failures in the report. A small error among successful tracks says little about a system that misses many attempts.

## The product is the feedback loop

For an athlete, the result should lead to a usable next action. For a coach, the same metric should mean the same thing next week. For an operator, the installation should be reproducible and the record auditable.

That suggests a practical product requirement: preserve the event time, measurement definition, installation profile, software version, validity state, and whether a value is measured or modeled. A missing track should be shown as unavailable rather than silently replaced by the previous result.

For MicroDetect, these are criteria for evaluating a sports-radar development project, not claims that existing industrial instruments are already validated sports products. A successful pilot should demonstrate one useful measurement under documented conditions before expanding the feature list.
